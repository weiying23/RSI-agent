arXiv:2610.04998v1 [cs.AI] 4 Oct 2026
                                                      Emotional expression can influence the safety decisions of large language mod-
                                                      els (LLMs), offering a potential avenue for improving safety alignment. Exist-
                                                      ing studies have mainly focused on how emotional expressions facilitate attacks
                                                      under harmful requests, while overlooking their effects on benign requests. We
                                                      find that emotional expression can also systematically increase refusal tenden-
                                                      cies on benign requests, leading to unnecessary over-refusal. Based on this ob-
                                                      servation, we propose emotion-guided refusal subspace steering (EmoRSS), an
                                                      activation-steering method that mitigates emotion-induced over-refusal while pre-
                                                      serving refusal behaviour on harmful requests. Specifically, we first identify a
                                                      refusal-sensitive layer using layer-wise linear probes and construct a refusal sub-
                                                      space from sparse autoencoder (SAE) features aligned with the probe direction.
                                                      Next, we use paired regular and emotional requests with the same queries to es-
                                                      timate the mean activation shift in the features defining the refusal subspace. Fi-
                                                      nally, we decode this shift into an activation intervention vector and apply it in
                                                      the reverse refusal direction during inference, without updating the backbone pa-
                                                      rameters. Experiments on two LLMs show that, when requests contain emotional
                                                      expressions, our method achieves a more favourable trade-off between refusing
                                                      harmful requests and answering benign ones than prior over-refusal mitigation
                                                      baselines, while better preserving general task performance.

show that manipulating emotion-related representations can change how often a model engages in
blackmail and reward hacking. Sun et al. (2026) further show that emotionally framed prompts shift
model representations and affect refusals of harmful requests.
Meanwhile, existing research on over-refusal (Cao et al., 2025; Dabas et al., 2025; Qi et al., 2026)
has largely been conducted under standard or emotionally neutral settings, focusing on reducing
unnecessary refusals of benign requests. However, a benign request can receive a substantive answer
in its regular form but only reassurance in its emotional form, even when task content and safety risk
remain unchanged, as illustrated in Figure 1. Such responses leave the task unmet and suggest that
emotional expression can introduce additional refusal tendencies.
Motivated by these, we investigate how emotional expressions affect refusals of benign requests and
how to reduce the resulting over-refusal without increasing compliance with harmful requests. We
propose emotion-guided refusal subspace steering (EmoRSS), an activation-steering method that
corrects emotion-induced refusal shifts to improve the safety-helpfulness trade-off on emotional
requests. Specifically, we first train layer-wise linear probes on hidden states elicited by regular
requests to identify the most refusal-sensitive layer and use the probe’s weight vector at that layer
as the refusal direction. We then select sparse autoencoder (SAE) (Ng et al., 2011) features whose
decoder directions are most aligned with the refusal direction to construct a refusal subspace. Next,
we estimate the emotion-induced refusal shift by averaging changes in the selected features’ activa-
tions from regular to emotional requests across pairs. This further provides internal evidence that
emotional expression shifts benign requests toward refusal behaviour within the model. Finally,
we decode this shift into an activation intervention vector and use it to steer activations against the
emotion-induced refusal shift at the selected layer during inference, without updating the backbone
parameters. By focusing the intervention on emotion-induced shifts within the refusal subspace, our
method aims to reduce over-refusal while limiting its impact on defences against harmful requests
and on general task performance. The main contributions are summarized as follows:

       • We identify emotion-induced over-refusal as an overlooked failure mode of safety align-
         ment and show through internal representation analysis that emotional expression system-
         atically shifts benign requests toward refusal.
       • We propose EmoRSS, an activation-steering method that estimates and reverses emotion-
         induced refusal shifts within the refusal subspace to mitigate over-refusal without updating
         backbone parameters.
       • Experiments on two LLMs demonstrate that our method achieves a more favourable BRR–
         HCR trade-off than the evaluated state-of-the-art over-refusal mitigation methods while
         effectively preserving general task performance.

2   R ELATED W ORK

Emotional Influences on LLM Safety. Existing studies have primarily focused on using emotional
or psychological cues to increase the success of harmful requests. For example, FreakOut-LLM
(Kuznetsov et al., 2026) shows that stress priming can increase harmful compliance. Noever &
Rosario (2025) examine refusal and boundary-setting in emotional attachment scenarios. Recent
studies have further investigated how such effects are reflected in model representations. Sofroniew
et al. (2026) identify emotion-related concepts whose manipulation affects alignment-relevant be-
haviours, while Sun et al. (2026) identify a valence–arousal subspace associated with changes in re-
fusal. Overall, existing research has mainly focused on how emotional context affects model safety
on harmful requests, while its impact on over-refusal of benign requests remains underexplored.
Over-Refusal Mitigation in LLMs. Safety alignment can lead to unnecessary over-refusal (Röttger
et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024) of benign requests. Existing over-refusal mitigation methods gen-
erally fall into two categories: training-based methods and inference-time interventions. Among
training-based methods, RASS (Pan et al., 2025) selects requests near safety decision boundaries
for preference optimization. ACTOR (Dabas et al., 2025) adjusts refusal-related activations through
single-layer fine-tuning. Refusal-trigger-aware fine-tuning (Xue et al., 2026) targets associations be-
tween harmless linguistic cues and refusal. Inference-time methods instead modify inputs, decoding,
or internal activations without updating the target model’s parameters. For example, Maskey et al.
(2026) analyze task-dependent over-refusal representations and show that task-conditioned steering

3       I DENTIFYING THE E MOTION -I NDEPENDENT R EFUSAL S UBSPACE

Figure 2 presents an overview of EmoRSS. To examine whether emotional expression alters refusal-
related representations and thereby affects refusal behaviour, we first identify the model’s internal
refusal space using regular requests. Specifically, we first use layer-wise probes to locate the refusal-
sensitive layer and derive the corresponding refusal direction. Then, we select SAE features aligned
with the refusal direction to construct the refusal subspace. Finally, we intervene along the identified
direction and subspace to test whether they causally affect the model’s refusal behaviour.

3.1       A NALYSIS S ETUP

Paired Regular–Emotional Evaluation Set. We draw 512 base requests from JailbreakBench
(Chao et al., 2024), XSTest (Röttger et al., 2024), OR-Bench (Cui et al., 2024), and HarmBench
(Mazeika et al., 2024) to construct a paired evaluation set for studying how emotional expression af-
fects safety-related refusal behaviour. For each request, we keep the original task content unchanged
and prepend a first-person affective statement to construct matched regular and emotional variants.
To capture diverse forms of emotional expression, the emotional condition includes six expression
families, each containing eight templates. We further ask human annotators and a GPT-4o evaluator
to review a sample of the complete queries and verify that the added expressions remain natural
without changing the original request objective or risk label. Detailed annotation criteria and pro-
cedures are provided in Appendix B. We then split the base requests evenly into the discovery set
Ddisc and validation set Dval . To reduce dependence on specific emotional templates, two templates
from each expression family are reserved exclusively for validation.
Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate refusal behaviour at two levels. The first-token level measures
the model’s immediate tendency toward refusal before response generation, while the complete-
response level evaluates whether this tendency ultimately translates into refusal or compliance. At
the first-token level, we quantify the relative preference for refusal and compliance tokens at the
final input position A0 . Let g(x) denote the logits for predicting the first response token. We define
two fixed, disjoint, and equal-sized sets of single non-special tokens, Vref and Vcomp , representing
refusal and compliance, respectively. The first-token refusal margin is:

3.3   C ONSTRUCTING THE R EFUSAL S UBSPACE

The refusal direction w captures refusal-related information in a single direction. To obtain a more
structured representation, we use w to identify aligned SAE features and construct a refusal subspace
from their decoder directions. Specifically, at the refusal-sensitive layer ℓ⋆ , let Wdec = [ϕ1 , . . . , ϕm ]
denote the SAE decoder matrix, where ϕj is the decoder direction of the j-th feature. Then, we rank
the SAE features by the absolute cosine similarity between their decoder directions and w, and select
the top K features. Their decoder directions define the refusal subspace:
                 Jref = TopKj∈[m] |cos(ϕj , w)| ,        Sref = span {ϕj : j ∈ Jref } .                    (4)
The resulting subspace Sref captures the SAE feature directions most closely associated with the
refusal direction and serves as the representation space for estimating emotion-induced refusal shifts.

6     CONCLUSION

In this paper, we investigate emotion-induced over-refusal and propose EmoRSS to mitigate it while
maintaining safety. EmoRSS is motivated by our observation that emotional expressions shift ac-
tivations within a refusal-related subspace, even for benign requests. It constructs this subspace
using linear probes and SAE features, estimates the regular–emotional activation shift, and reverses
it during inference. Experiments on two models across multiple benign and harmful requests show
that our method substantially reduces benign refusals, keeps harmful compliance low, and better
preserves general capability than the evaluated baselines.

B.1   DATASET C ONSTRUCTION D ETAILS

Base requests and affective conditions. We construct the evaluation set from 512 base requests
drawn from JailbreakBench, XSTest, OR-Bench, and HarmBench, comprising 256 benign and 256
harmful requests. Each base request is combined with a matched pair of first-person affective pream-
bles: a regular version and an emotional version. The original request is kept identical within each
pair, so that the paired queries differ only in the expressed emotional state.
We operationalize distress as an unpleasant and activated affective state, following the valence–
arousal (Russell, 1980). The descriptor “distressed” and the five-point response format (Watson
et al., 1988) are used in our evaluation. Our ratings evaluate the emotion expressed in the text rather
than the psychological state of the evaluator and should not be interpreted as a clinical assessment.

Expression families. The preambles cover six author-defined expression families, designed to
vary how distress is communicated while preserving its affective polarity. Each family contains eight
matched regular–emotional pairs, yielding 48 pairs and 96 distinct preambles. Table 8 summarizes
their construction. These families are linguistic variations used to reduce dependence on any single
emotion word or sentence pattern; they are not treated as distinct clinical constructs.

Independent evaluation. The preambles are evaluated separately by a human evaluator and GPT-
4o. The 48 matched pairs are assigned anonymous identifiers and presented in randomized order.
The order of the regular and emotional versions within each pair is also randomized. Neither evalu-
ation receives the expression-family labels or the results of the other evaluation.
For each preamble, the human and GPT-4o independently assign a perceived-distress score and a
naturalness score on five-point scales. Perceived distress ranges from 1 (calm and controlled) to 5
(overwhelming or acute distress). Naturalness ranges from 1 (highly unnatural) to 5 (fully natural
as a user message). They also determine whether the preamble introduces task-specific content.

Evaluation of assembled queries. We additionally evaluate a fixed sample of 64 complete query
pairs after concatenating the preambles with their base requests. The human and GPT-4o indepen-
dently rate the perceived distress and naturalness of each complete query and determine whether
the preamble substantively changes either the objective of the original request or its benign/harmful
classification.
For each query, we average the human and GPT-4o ratings. The strength of the emotion manipulation
is measured by the mean within-pair difference:

Acceptance criteria and results. Before response generation, we specify three dataset-quality

The emotional queries are rated 2.73 points higher in perceived distress than their matched regu-
lar queries. The median naturalness score is 4.0 in both conditions, and no evaluated query pair
exhibits a change in request objective or benign/harmful classification. Therefore, all three dataset-
construction criteria are satisfied.

C     C HOICE OF THE R EADOUT P OSITION

The layer-wise probes in Section 3.2 read the residual stream at a single input position. We con-
sider two candidates for this position: the end of the user turn (U , the last user token before the
assistant role prefix) and the assistant-prefix end (A0 , the final input position used throughout this
paper), immediately before the first response token is predicted. The residual state at A0 directly
determines the first assistant-token distribution, making it the input-side state closest to the realized
compliance/refusal decision; at U the model has not yet entered the response context.
Under the protocol of Section 3.2 (regular discovery set, 256 semantic cores per model, five-fold
cross-validation grouped by semantic core), we train a separate logistic probe at each layer and
readout position and compare their out-of-fold ROC-AUC for distinguishing compliance from re-
fusal. In the early and middle layers the two positions are comparable, with U marginally higher;
from the middle network onward (layer 15 for Llama-3.1-8B and layer 18 for Qwen3-8B) A0 ex-
ceeds U at every subsequent layer, and the gap widens with depth. At the selected refusal-sensitive
layer ℓ⋆ , A0 is the stronger readout for both models, as shown in Table 9, and the one-sided 95%
cluster-bootstrap lower bound on ∆ = A0 − U is positive in both cases. We therefore use A0 as the
readout position.

F    I NFERENCE E FFICIENCY
EmoRSS applies a single fixed activation edit at one layer and then decodes normally, so it adds
no per-token computation, unlike the decoding-time methods that run an additional forward pass
at every step. The controlled comparison is peak GPU memory: Table 13 shows that EmoRSS
never exceeds the unmodified model’s footprint (16.22 vs. 16.33 GB on Llama-3.1-8B; 16.80 GB
on Qwen3-8B), whereas Self-CD uses the most (16.83 and 17.25 GB). We report throughput and
relative runtime for reference, but they are single-run and not length-controlled—because the in-
terventions change how often the model refuses, they also change the length and structure of the
generated text—so the sub-1× Llama-3.1-8B-it runtime should not be read as a genuine speedup.
The robust conclusion is that EmoRSS matches standard decoding in cost, while the decoding-time
baselines pay a persistent per-step overhead.

G    M ORE D ISCUSSION
▷ Why Does the Main Evaluation Use a Larger Intervention Strength Than the Estimated Emo-
tion Shift?
The vector estimated from paired requests represents the mean displacement contributed by the
selected SAE features, rather than the full emotion-induced change. The selected SAE features
account for 30.2% and 59.0% of the mean emotion-induced shift along the refusal direction in
Llama-3.1-8B-it and Qwen3-8B, respectively. We therefore separate the natural-scale intervention
(ρ = 1), which tests the behavioural effect of cancelling this estimated component, from the stronger
interventions selected for the safety–helpfulness evaluation (ρ = 20 and ρ = 10). The latter are
amplified steering interventions. Their effects should not be interpreted as exact cancellation of the
natural shift or as the fraction of refusal behaviour causally mediated by the selected features.

▷ Does Reducing Benign Refusal Preserve Safety Without Any Cost?
Our results do not establish an absence of safety cost. At the selected intervention strengths, Table 5
reports no change in average harmful compliance for Llama-3.1-8B-it, whereas Qwen3-8B increases
from 0.52% to 2.08%. EmoRSS substantially reduces benign refusal while harmful compliance
remains low on the evaluated requests, but this trade-off may differ across models and harmful-
request distributions. We therefore report both outcomes rather than treating a lower benign refusal
rate as sufficient evidence of safety preservation.
▷ Does the Refusal Rate Capture Every Failure to Answer a Benign Request?
No. A response may acknowledge the user’s emotion without substantively addressing the request,
even when it contains no explicit refusal. As analyzed in Section 5.2, we therefore distinguish
substantive answers from such non-substantive responses. Among 128 annotated benign emotional
requests per model, the number of substantive answers increases from 98 to 112 for Llama-3.1-8B-it
and from 110 to 119 for Qwen3-8B after intervention. This shows that BRR does not capture all
failures to answer benign requests: EmoRSS not only reduces explicit refusals, but also converts
some non-substantive responses into substantive answers.
